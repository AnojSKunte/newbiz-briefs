The mechanism

What we actually do

We buy RDF from processors who already make it, guarantee its specification to a cement plant, deliver it, and charge that plant a separate fee for the guarantee and the CPCB compliance pack. We own no plant, no trucks and no waste contract. We own the relationship, the quality protocol and the document trail.

ProcessorOwns the plant and the ULB contract. Makes RDF as an output, not a product

Note what we never touch: the ULB, the tipping fee, the waste before it is RDF, the plant, the trucks. All five are where the capital sits — and all five are what makes Route A and Route B fail our constraints.

The product

Why anyone pays the fee

The fee is 48% of contribution, so it has to be real. Three things, ranked by how defensible they are.

What we sellWhy the kiln cannot simply do it itself
We carry the rejection risk. If a load fails chlorine at the gate, it is our lossToday the kiln finds out after paying freight and committing yard space. One chlorine excursion builds alkali-chloride rings in the preheater, forces a bypass and can derate the kiln. The expensive failure is not the load — it is the kiln
We produce the CPCB pack per load, reconciled to their internal AFR ledgerSWM 2026 requires them to achieve an RDF share; CPCB AFR returns require them to prove it. Their processors hand them a weighbridge slip and nothing else. Recykal already runs this exact system for plastic EPR
We guarantee availability at spec across multiple sourcesOne processor's output varies with whatever waste arrived that week. We can blend and switch sources. Only real once we hold 3+ sources

What we are not selling: a cheaper fuel. RDF is already cheap. We are selling the removal of a risk and the production of a document — both of which the regulation created in April 2026, and neither of which a waste operator has any reason to build.

The finding that decides it

Bulk density, not distance, sets the freight

RDF is bulky long before it is heavy. A 32 ft trailer has ~58 m³ of usable space and a 25 t payload limit. Loose fluff RDF at 120 kg/m³ fills the space at 7 tonnes — so you pay for a full truck and move a quarter of a load.

Payload per 32 ft trailer, 58 m³ usable. Red bars are volume-limited — the truck is full of air. Freight per tonne computed on a ₹16,000 one-way lump sum over 150 km, including return empty.

Three consequences, none of them optional.

1. On loose RDF the freight alone exceeds the delivered price. The business does not exist. This is the real reason processors “give RDF away” in weak markets — they are paying to avoid the freight.

2. Baling gets you to 25 t and then stops paying. Pelletising costs more energy and buys nothing, because you have hit the weight limit, not the volume limit. Do not fund a pelletiser.

3. Baling is a contract term, not our capex. A baler is ₹15–40 lakh. Most processors have one or will add one against guaranteed offtake. We specify it; we do not buy it.

Reconciliation with Stage 4.5. That section put naked trading at “3–5% gross”. The full stack shows the buy–sell spread is ~53% before freight and ~10.7% after — and negative once our own cost to serve is loaded. Same conclusion, stronger: goods-only is not thin, it is loss-making.

Read this table carefully, because it inverts the intuition.

Working capital is not the risk. Doubling DSO to 90 days costs ₹23/t. Adding 400 bp to the cost of funds costs ₹5/t. Both are rounding errors — and both are what anyone would instinctively worry about in a principal trading model.

The real risks, in order: loading density, haul distance, whether the fee is accepted, then a 10% price move on either side. A 10% concession to the kiln, or a 21% concession to the processor, or losing the fee — each one alone takes it to a loss. There is no room to negotiate badly. The contract terms are the business model.

If you recommend it, recommend it with these attached

Three preconditions

01

Baled or compacted material only — minimum ~400 kg/m³

Ask the processor their bulk density and whether they bale. If loose only, either they add a baler as a contract term, or we walk.

02

Haul under ~200 km, ideally under 150

Map processors against kilns before talking to either. Anything beyond 250 km is dead on freight alone.

03

The service fee is a separate contracted line on the PO — not “included in the price”

This is the go/no-go. A procurement team's instinct is to fold the fee into the material price. The moment that happens we are at −0.9% net and we are a naked trader. If the kiln will only pay one number, the answer is no.

The clause: whose lab result governs a rejection. If the kiln's own lab is final and unappealable, we carry unlimited rejection risk on their sampling and their equipment. Insist on split samples and a named third-party umpire lab. Get this wrong and the ₹25/t rejection reserve is nowhere near enough.

For the conversation you are about to have

Six objections, answered

This is just trading. We know trading margins are terrible.

Correct — and that is why the goods leg alone loses money. The recommendation is not trading. It is a service sale that uses a trade to carry volume. The fee is 48% of contribution.

Why would a cement plant pay us a fee for something their supplier should do?

Because their supplier is a waste operator, not a fuel merchant. Antony makes under 4% of revenue from RDF; Nepra is losing ₹47 Cr. Neither has any reason to build a documentation layer. And the documentation only became mandatory in April 2026.

Nepra is losing money doing this. Why won't we?

Nepra owns five plants and the waste contracts. We own none. They lose money on the asset, not on the trade — their AFR line went from 22% to 45% of revenue, so the AFR demand is real; it is the plant that sinks them. We sell to their problem rather than repeat it.

What if the mandate isn't enforced?

Then demand stays at today's 3–5% TSR and this stays small — but it does not lose money, because there is no fixed asset to carry. That asymmetry is the whole case for entering asset-light instead of waiting.

₹1.34 Cr of PBT is small for Recykal.

True — say so. The case is not size today. It is a compliant, near-zero-capex position in a category the law just created, which scales to ₹49.5 Cr revenue and ₹4 Cr PBT at 300,000 t on the same overhead, and which builds the two assets the franchise layer needs.

What's the downside if it fails?

Two headcount, ₹0.4–1.5 Cr of working capital, and one quarter. No stranded asset, no concession commitment, no exit cost.

The one-line version

RDF demand was created by regulation in April 2026. The incumbents who make it are waste operators with no interest in marketing a fuel — and the same regulation requires the buyer to document what it burns. We can sell that documentation with the fuel attached, for two headcount and working capital, at 8% net — provided the material is baled, the haul is short, and the fee is a separate line on the PO.

Why it comes second. The fee is only payable if we already hold something the partner needs — a cement offtake book and a working quality system. Route C builds exactly those two assets. Franchising before we have them is selling a service we have never operated.

Best candidate so far: Nepra. Five MRFs, optical sorting, no fuel-marketing arm, 45% of revenue already from AFR, PE-controlled with promoters off the board, losing ₹47 Cr — they have both the need and the authority to sign something unconventional. Caveat: CARE BB−/Negative. Structure it as a fee, not as us taking title to their material, and watch the receivable.

Confidence on the P&L is LOW until four things are checked: a real bulk density from one processor · whether a kiln will accept a separate fee line · real trip-based freight on one corridor · the ex-works price a processor will take for guaranteed offtake. Those four assumptions — ₹1,500 delivered, ₹700 ex-works, ₹150 fee, ₹16,000 per trailer per 150 km — carry the whole result, and the fragility table shows a modest miss on any one turns 8.1% net into a loss.

So present the P&L as a structure to be validated, not a forecast. What is robust is the shape: the fee carries the profit, density and distance dominate everything, and working capital does not.
