Project 04 · RDF from solid waste · End-to-end business case

The fuel is the by-product. The fee is the business.

RDF looks like a fuel trade and behaves like a waste-service contract. That one distinction decides whether an asset-light, 5%-net, fully-compliant entry is possible — and it is, but not in the shape most people assume.

The verdict

Enter as a principal aggregator with a compliance-and-quality service layer on top. Do not build a plant, and do not trade RDF naked.

Not the plant
The money sits in the tipping fee — which needs a 15–25 year ULB concession and ₹25–45 Cr. That is the incumbents' game. Fails the capex constraint outright.
Not naked trading
Low-value, high-freight commodity. Goods-only is loss-making once our own cost to serve is loaded. This is the Buyofuel trap — ₹100 Cr revenue at 4.25% gross, still losing money in year five.
What works
Take RDF on our books for revenue scale and charge separately for the two things the buyer cannot do itself — guaranteeing spec and producing the CPCB paperwork that proves it. 8.1% net, of which the fee is 48% of contribution.

01What RDF actually is

The combustible fraction left after organics, recyclables and inerts are pulled out of mixed municipal waste, then shredded and dried. It is a residual product — and the market prices it like one.

Three terms that get used interchangeably, and shouldn't

TermWhat it isCommercial consequence
MSWRaw mixed municipal wasteNot a fuel. A disposal liability someone pays to remove
RDFProcessed combustible fraction, loosely specifiedSells, but at a heavy discount to coal
SRFSolid Recovered Fuel — same material to a tighter certified spec, with GCV, chlorine and mercury declared per lotCommands a premium. Required for main-burner feed
Why this table is the whole strategy in miniature

The RDF → SRF step is a quality upgrade, and that is where value gets added. Same molecules, higher price, because the specification is declared and guaranteed. Everything we recommend is a version of moving material up this ladder — and MoHUA has already written the ladder into the regulation as Grades I, II and III.

What is in it, and what is not

In the RDFNot in it — and where it goes insteadShare of MSW
Multi-layer packaging, chips and biscuit wrappersKitchen and food waste → compost or biogas~50%
Textile scraps, rags, old clothingPET, HDPE, cardboard, metal, glass → sold as recyclables, worth far more15–20%
Soiled and laminated paper, tetrapakSand, grit, stones, C&D fines → landfill20–25%
Rubber, foam, leather, thin filmsSanitary waste → separate stream under SWM 2026—
The two things that actually govern the trade

1. Chlorine is the gate, not calorific value. PVC and food salt build alkali-chloride rings in the kiln preheater, force a bypass, corrode ductwork and can cap output. A high-GCV RDF that fails chlorine is worthless. GCV ranges 2,500–5,000 kcal/kg; Indian MSW-derived RDF typically lands 3,000–3,500 as-received.

2. Grade III is defined as usable only after blending. A grade whose definition requires a service implies a service provider. That is written into the regulation, not invented by us.

Why owning either plant still fails

Mixed MSW: a 300 TPD plant runs ~99,000 t/yr → ~₹10.3 Cr revenue, ~₹1.3 Cr contribution against ₹25–45 Cr capex. That is why these are government-tendered monopolies with built-in escalation, not merchant businesses.

Pre-segregated dry: the 52.8% contribution above assumes you are paid to take the waste. Strip that assumption and it inverts — §03. Nepra runs exactly this model and lost ₹47 Cr on ₹131 Cr of revenue.

03The dry-waste route — who pays whom?

And this is exactly what happened to Nepra

Their contracted, tipping-fee-bearing work fell from 41% of revenue to 20% in one year. They drifted from Case 1 toward Case 3 — from being paid to take material, to paying for it.

Our Case 3 arithmetic says −53.6% contribution. Nepra's actual net margin is −36% (they still hold some Case 1 work). The model and the reality agree.

One nuance worth knowing: a plant can hold both at once — contracted volume that pays, topped up with bought material to fill idle capacity. That is the trap. Underutilisation pushes you to buy, and buying is the losing case. Nepra's granules plant ran at 18% of capacity.

The conclusion, and it is the one that shapes everything after

The tipping fee is not a revenue line you can go and get. It is an asset — a collection contract — and it is the whole difference between +52.8% and −53.6%.

We cannot buy that asset within our constraints. So we should not own the plant. We should sell a service to whoever does — and the best candidate is a Case-1 holder whose realisation is falling. That describes Nepra precisely.

04The mandate

SWM Rules 2026, notified under the Environment (Protection) Act, in force since 1 April 2026, superseding the 2016 Rules.

ProvisionWhat it does
RDF substitutionCement and waste-to-energy plants on solid fuel must replace it with RDF — 5% rising to 15% over six years
Four-stream segregationWet · Dry · Sanitary · Special Care, at source. Creates the clean dry stream
Landfill restrictionLimited to non-recyclable inerts. Higher fees for unsegregated waste
Legacy dumpsitesTime-bound biomining, quarterly reporting. A large, dated service market
CPCB AFR returnsMonthly and annual utilisation returns, reconciled to the plant's own AFR ledger
Why this mandate should work where the biomass one didn't

Push and pull arrive together. The kiln gets a fuel obligation at the same moment landfill is closed off and segregation is enforced. The waste must go somewhere; the kiln must take something.

Biomass co-firing has been mandated for years and runs at ~3.8% of requirement — it obliged a buyer without guaranteeing a price, and never had the second half.

But the near-term bite is weak — three facts
  • Cement TSR already averages 3–5%; UltraTech is at 5.7%. The 5% floor is close to free for leaders, a small stretch for the average.
  • TSR counts all alternative fuels — biomass, tyres, hazardous waste. A plant at 5% TSR may burn very little RDF. The RDF-specific share is the number to get, and it is not published.
  • Nobody has yet named a penalty for missing 5%. Until someone does, treat demand as potential, not contracted.

Implication: the window is years 2–4, as the ramp to 15% starts to bind. Build relationships cheaply now; do not deploy capital.

What that means

The 15% mandate needs roughly three-quarters of every tonne of RDF India could theoretically make. Demand is not the constraint — capture, quality and freight are.

And the ceiling is not just about waste volume: chlorine saturation caps RDF-based TSR at about 12–15%, which is exactly where the mandate stops. That is not a coincidence.

The one pattern in this table that decides the strategy

The two companies selling contracted services are profitable. The two selling material are not. Antony at +10.5% net against Nepra at −36%, on the same underlying waste stream. Buyofuel confirms it on biomass.

This is the strongest evidence we have for why the entry must be principal plus service, not trading alone. The material leg buys revenue scale; it cannot buy margin. Every company here that tried has failed.

The strategic read

Every incumbent owns assets and holds ULB concessions. They are not a competitive threat to an asset-light entrant — they are the supply base and the franchise counterparty.

They share one weakness: they are waste operators, not fuel merchants. They sell RDF as an output to get rid of it. Nobody hands the cement plant a guaranteed specification with auditable paperwork attached.

Two live openings. Re Sustainability is mid-sale — unusually willing to sign offtake volume, unusually unwilling to start a new service line. Nepra is distressed and 45% AFR-dependent — five MRFs, optical sorting, no fuel-marketing arm, and they need AFR realisation to rise. Call both; treat Nepra's BB− as a receivable to watch, not a reason to skip them.

The line that is the whole argument

The service fee is ₹150 of the ₹310 contribution — 48%. Strip it out and the goods leg alone is −₹16/t.

So this is not a trade with a service attached. It is a service with a trade attached — the trade carries revenue scale, and the fee is where the profit lives.

Three preconditions — not preferences
  • Baled material, ≥400 kg/m³. Loose RDF fills a trailer at 7 t, so freight is ₹2,299/t — more than the material is worth. Baled hits the 25 t weight limit at ₹640/t. Baling is a contract term on the processor, not our capex.
  • Haul under ~200 km. At 300 km the model is −31%.
  • The fee is a separate contracted line on the PO. Fold it into the material price and we are at −0.9%. This is the go/no-go.

Counter-intuitively, working capital is not the risk — doubling DSO to 90 days costs ₹23/t. Density and distance are.

The lesson we already paid for

Buyofuel does exactly the first row of the table above — ₹100 Cr revenue, 4.25% gross against a 16.3% peer median, still loss-making in year five, funded at 15–18% by venture debt. It never built the service layer.

The single most valuable thing we know about this category is what that company got wrong.

The internal conflict worth naming now

SWM 2026 mandates RDF into cement kilns. It does not mandate biomass. Every point of kiln thermal demand filled by mandated RDF is a point unavailable to Project 03's agri-residue briquettes — and RDF has the regulation on its side.

Resolution: RDF wins in cement. Keep agri-residue pointed at industrial boilers, where Project 01 showed the ₹/Mcal case is strongest against furnace oil and piped gas rather than coal. Two projects, two buyer sets, no cannibalisation.

And the three questions that decide whether any of this becomes a business
  • Is the SWM 2026 penalty real? Without it, demand is potential, not contracted.
  • Will a cement plant pay a separately-lined fee for guaranteed spec and documentation? That is the entire margin defence.
  • Will a processor discount for guaranteed offtake, and will they bale? That is the entire spread.

All three are answerable by phone in two weeks. None requires capital.
