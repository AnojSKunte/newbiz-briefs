Q1The mandate — RDF-specific for cement

From the primary source this time: SWM Rules 2026, gazette notification S.O. 388(E), notified 27 January 2026, in force 1 April 2026. Rule 11, table on page 89.

“The industrial units utilizing solid fuel shall use Refuse-Derived Fuel or Segregated Combustible Fraction or agri-residue, as the case may be, as per the table given below.”

The operative phrase is “as the case may be, as per the table” — each fuel is tied to its own row, and each row names its own permitted end use.

#Combustible fractionCalorific valueIntended useDistance
1SCF or agri-residue> 1,500 kcal/kg netWtE plants, or industries with boilers100 km
2RDF Grade I> 4,500 kcal/kg netDirect co-processing in cement kilns400 km
3RDF Grade II3,750–4,500 kcal/kg netDirect co-processing in cement kilns400 km
4RDF Grade III3,000–3,750 kcal/kg netCo-processing directly or after processing with other waste materials in cement kilns400 km
So the answer is: fuel-specific by unit type

For a cement kiln the table names only RDF Grades I/II/III. Agri-residue and SCF sit in row 1, whose intended use is WtE plants and boilers — not kilns.

Which means a cement plant's existing TSR from tyres, hazardous waste or biomass does not discharge this obligation. The 6% must be MSW-derived RDF meeting a named grade. That makes the demand case stronger and more urgent than my earlier read, not weaker.

And one correction that weakens a recommendation of mine

I said “MoHUA defines Grade III as usable only after blending” and built Option B — the upgrade play — on it. The gazette says Grade III is for co-processing “directly or after processing with other waste materials.” Direct use is permitted.

Option B's regulatory hook is therefore weaker than I claimed. Blending is an option, not a requirement. It needs a commercial justification instead — and the price-to-calorific gradient in Q2 is a better one.

A finding that belongs to Project 03, not this one

I wrote that SWM 2026 mandates RDF and not biomass, so RDF wins the kiln slot. Half right. Agri-residue is named in the rules — but for WtE plants and industrial boilers within 100 km, not cement kilns.

So agri-residue now has regulation-backed mandated demand in boilers — which is exactly where Project 01 already showed the ₹/Mcal case was strongest against furnace oil and gas. That is a new demand driver we did not have. Worth acting on separately.

There is a real price-to-calorific-value gradient

₹400/t below 2,500 kcal. ₹1,500/t at 3,000–3,500 kcal. That gradient is the first hard evidence for the upgrade play — and a better foundation for Option B than the regulatory reading I got wrong.

But none of it is usable as-is

Not one quote states whether it is ex-works or delivered. That single ambiguity is decisive, because our entire margin lives between the two.

  • If ₹1,500/t is ex-works, delivered at 150 km is ~₹2,250/t and our buy price must be far below ₹1,500.
  • If ₹1,500/t is delivered, our buy price must be under ~₹800 and the spread is thin.

My model assumed buy ₹700 ex-works and sell ₹1,500 delivered. The public evidence cannot confirm that pair — and no amount of desk research will.

The open item is not “what is the RDF price.” It is “what is the ex-works price and the delivered price on the same corridor, on the same day.” Two phone calls.

The gap between the two radii is the whole strategic question

The rules oblige kilns within 400 km of an RDF plant. Our economics work to roughly 200 km. The 200–400 km band is obligated but not economically self-servable.

That is either the opportunity — someone must solve it, and density plus route density is how — or the trap, if kilns simply refuse to pay for distance. It is the second question to ask on the first call.

How to present the upside numbers honestly

Configurations reaching 22–33% net exist in the model — sale ₹1,800 plus a ₹300 fee at 100 km, for instance. Do not present those as a forecast. They are what the model returns if a ₹1,800–2,000 delivered price and a ₹700 ex-works buy coexist on one corridor — and that pair is exactly what Q2 shows is unverified.

The defensible framing: the business is viable inside a narrow envelope of price and distance, and we can test whether that envelope exists for roughly zero cost in two weeks.

The structural dead zones — and a correction to my own sizing

Delhi, 11,000 TPD: no cement kilns in NCR. Nearest are Kotputli (Rajasthan) and Bilaspur (HP). North Delhi Municipal Corp had to float an all-India tender for its Bhalswa biomining RDF precisely because it had no local offtake.

Mumbai, 8,000 TPD: no kilns within economic reach.

India's two largest waste sources are commercially stranded for RDF. Any sizing that multiplies national MSW by a yield factor is wrong for exactly this reason — and mine did. Geography, not volume, is the binding constraint.

Recommendation — Hyderabad into Nalgonda and Gulbarga
  • The best waste-to-kiln pairing in India. 8,000–9,000 TPD of MSW with cement capacity at ~100 km and ~200 km. Nothing else comes close on both dimensions at once.
  • 100 km puts us in the ₹1,145/t breakeven band — the most forgiving row in the chart above, and the difference between a viable and an unviable model.
  • Two clusters off one supply base. Competitive tension between buyers, and a fallback if one kiln's spec proves impossible.
  • Hyderabad already processes at scale, so baled, spec-consistent material is plausible rather than aspirational.
  • Recykal is headquartered here. For a play whose entire first phase is phone calls and one truck, zero-cost site access matters more than it looks.

Second choice: Nagpur → Chandrapur. 150 km, one clean corridor, much smaller volume. A controlled second test, not the opening move. Do not start in Delhi or Mumbai despite the waste — the kilns are not there.

What to verify before committing to the corridor

  • Which Hyderabad-area processors bale, and to what bulk density
  • Which Nalgonda and Gulbarga plants are below 6% RDF — those are the buyers with a problem
  • Actual road distance plant-by-plant, not cluster centroid
  • Whether Telangana SPCB has issued any Rule 11 enforcement — the first real test of whether the penalty bites
